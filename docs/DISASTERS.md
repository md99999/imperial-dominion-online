# Disasters

Drought, insects and flood: three things that arrive without warning during a turn, destroy a
share of one thing, and pass. Nobody sends them and nobody can fight them. They are weather, in
the way [barbarians](BARBARIANS.md) are weather, but they fall on a different part of the board.

## What they do

When a turn is spent there is a chance one lands. Each takes the same share, and each takes it
from somewhere different:

| Disaster | What it destroys |
| --- | --- |
| Drought | Farmsteads, burned across the fields |
| Insects | Stored grain, eaten where it stands |
| Flood | Homesteads, swept away |

A destroyed building hands its acre back to wilderness, exactly as demolishing it would. The land
is still yours; what stood on it is not. Nothing here takes land, troops or siege weapons.

The struck ruler is told plainly:

> The river has broken its banks. 84 homesteads are swept away, and the acres go back to
> wilderness.

It also reaches the gazette, because an empire that lost a tenth of its granaries this evening is
worth knowing about. It is the one public signal in the game that somebody is weaker than they
were this morning and nobody chose to make them so.

## Who they visit, and when

**Anybody.** This is the whole difference from barbarians, and it is the reason both exist.
Barbarians only visit the top three, so an empire in the middle of the field can spend a round
knowing exactly what its stores will be worth tomorrow. A disaster is the game telling it
otherwise. That is what makes a ruler keep a margin rather than running every granary to the last
bushel.

**Never during a grace period.** Not the crown truce a new empire holds, not the relief a ruined
empire is given, and not the board-wide grace after a reset.

This is the opposite of the barbarian rule, deliberately. Barbarians ignore a truce because a
truce protects against *players*, and barbarians are not players. But barbarians only strike
empires that have already climbed to the top three, which no empire does while still under its
opening truce, so the rule never actually bites. Disasters strike anybody, so it would — and an
empire that loses 7% of its homesteads on its first day has been handed a worse start than the
ruler beside it for no reason it could have played around. A grace period is a promise that
nothing will happen to you yet. A flood is something happening to you.

**Never two at once.** One disaster settles per batch of turns, whatever else the dice say. Two
together reads as the game being broken rather than the game being hard, and a ruler who loses
farmsteads and grain in the same order cannot tell which loss came from where.

## The odds

One in sixty turns, by default, which at ten turns a day is a little under one a week. Both the
odds and the share are settings, and the league governs them along with every other rule, so no
member can quietly turn off the weather for its own board.

The odds are expressed as one-in-N rather than as a percentage, because that is how they were
set. One in sixty is not a whole number of percent, and rounding it to 2% would be a different
game to no purpose.

## Why a share rather than a number

The same reason barbarians take a share: a flat loss punishes a small empire and tickles a large
one. A share scales with what is there, and can never take everything, because 93% of something
is still something.

The share is floored rather than rounded up, which means the smallest empires are the last to
feel it. An empire with fourteen farmsteads loses none to a 7% drought, and rather than announce
a disaster that destroyed nothing, no disaster happened.

## A drought is not cosmetic

If a drought burns down farmsteads on the second of ten turns, the remaining eight turns produce
what the empire can *actually* produce, not what it could have produced that morning. The loss
feeds back into the turn loop as it happens.

The same goes the other way: insects that empty the granaries can start a famine in the same
handful of turns, because the loss is taken before starvation is worked out. Losing your grain
and then watching your peasants flee is not two bugs. It is one disaster, followed by its
consequence.

## What they are not

Not an attack. There is no battle report, no defence, and nothing to build against.
Fortifications and an army at home make no difference.

That is deliberate, for the same reason it is deliberate with barbarians: the moment weather can
be defended against it becomes another military calculation, and the game already has one of
those.
